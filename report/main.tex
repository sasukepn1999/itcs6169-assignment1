\section{Final Result}
\label{sec:result}

Our final model reaches \textbf{93.1$\pm$0.8\%} accuracy on the test set (seeds: 93.5, 92.0 and 93.8\%), compared with 42.3\% for the starter network. All numbers are the mean and standard deviation over three seeds.

\textbf{Validation strategy.} We split the 2,400 labelled training images once into 1,920 images for training and 480 for validation, with the same seeded split as the starter notebook. Every design decision is made on this validation set, and the test set is only used for the final models. Since validation accuracy fluctuates from epoch to epoch, the best epoch gives an optimistic estimate, so we compare models by the average accuracy over the last 10 epochs. Our selected configuration reaches \textbf{93.7$\pm$0.5\%}.

\textbf{Test evaluation.} Our final model also uses FixMatch~\cite{fixmatch} on the 400 unlabelled images of \texttt{test2}. For an honest test accuracy, we train it on the training and validation images only, so it never sees the test images (neither labels nor pixels).

\textbf{Code and checkpoints.} The code is on \href{https://github.com/sasukepn1999/itcs6169-assignment1}{GitHub} and the checkpoints on \href{https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1q_nvnQqREGNsdmdg47nAFXf_cUVyYREe?usp=sharing}{Google Drive}. We provide two versions of the final model with the same recipe: the \emph{clean-test} version, trained on train+val, gives the test accuracy above, while the \emph{submitted} version is also trained on the labelled test images and produces our \texttt{test2} predictions (it has no test score, since no labelled image is left).

\section{Our Secret Recipe}
\label{sec:recipe}

Our final model is an EfficientNet-B0~\cite{efficientnet} initialised from ImageNet-1k~\cite{imagenet} weights. We replace the last linear layer with a 16-way classifier and fine-tune all layers. The network takes $200\times200$ grayscale images, replicated to three channels and normalised with the ImageNet statistics. During training, we apply random-resized cropping, random translation and horizontal flipping. We train for 200 epochs with Adam (batch size 64), with a peak learning rate of $10^{-4}$ that decays to zero with a cosine schedule, and keep the last-epoch weights. In addition to the cross-entropy loss on labelled images, we apply FixMatch~\cite{fixmatch} on the unlabelled \texttt{test2} images: the prediction on a weakly augmented view becomes a pseudo-label when its confidence exceeds 0.95, and the model learns to predict this label on a strongly augmented view (RandAugment~\cite{randaugment} and Cutout~\cite{cutout}).

\textbf{Which choices mattered?} As shown in Table~\ref{tab:journey}, three choices account for most of the improvement: data augmentation combined with longer training, which removes the overfitting of the baseline (+16 points), a deeper network (+15 points) and ImageNet pretraining (+10 points). More labels and FixMatch add a few more points. Finally, we keep every input in grayscale to avoid a colour shortcut (Sec.~\ref{sec:failure}).

\section{Experimental Journey}
\label{sec:journey}

\textbf{Dataset.} The dataset is balanced (150 training and 25 test images per class), so no re-balancing is needed. Two observations shaped our design: images are much larger than the $64\times64$ input of the starter network, and Flower is the only class stored in colour, so a model could recognise it from colour alone.

\textbf{Baseline.} The starter network (one convolution layer, 58K parameters) reaches 49.6\% validation and 42.3\% test accuracy. Its training loss drops to 0.12 while the validation loss rises after six epochs, so we first tackle overfitting.

\textbf{Augmentation and training length.} We add standard augmentations one at a time. Random translation helps the most and almost closes the gap between training and validation loss, while random-resized cropping and flipping help less. In contrast, rotation, colour jitter and random erasing do not help. Since the model is then still improving at the end of training, we combine the three useful augmentations and train longer, until accuracy saturates at about 200 epochs.

\textbf{Learning-rate schedule.} With a constant learning rate, the model still improves slowly after 500 epochs, which suggests decreasing the learning rate during training. A cosine schedule with a larger peak learning rate ($5\times10^{-3}$) reaches higher accuracy in 200 epochs than a constant one in 600.

\textbf{Resolution.} Larger inputs help the starter network, and $200\times200$ gives the best accuracy. Interestingly, $256\times256$ was the best size before we fixed overfitting but the worst afterwards.

\textbf{Architecture.} At this point, the training loss of the starter network stays near 1.0, i.e., the network is too small to fit the augmented data. We therefore compare seven standard CNNs trained from scratch. All reach 81--84\% except ConvNeXt-Tiny~\cite{convnext} (74.5\%), which likely needs more data. We keep EfficientNet-B0, which matches ResNet-50~\cite{resnet} with $6\times$ fewer parameters.

\textbf{Self-supervised learning.} Since the deeper networks overfit again, we add a self-supervised loss as a regulariser: rotation prediction~\cite{rotnet}, SimCLR~\cite{simclr}, SimSiam~\cite{simsiam} or masked image modelling~\cite{mae}. None of them clearly helps, whether applied to the training or the validation images.

\textbf{More labels and FixMatch.} On the test set, training EfficientNet-B0 (from scratch) on train+val instead of the training split alone improves test accuracy from 81.8\% to 84.8\%. FixMatch on the unlabelled test images further reaches 86.9\%, although its pseudo-label accuracy drops from 95\% to 87\% late in training (confirmation bias).

\textbf{ImageNet pretraining.} Finally, initialising EfficientNet-B0 from ImageNet weights gives the largest single gain, from 83.5\% to 93.7\%. A small learning rate ($10^{-4}$) works best, while larger ones degrade accuracy, probably because they overwrite the pretrained features.

\section{Failure Analysis}
\label{sec:failure}

\textbf{Keeping the colour of the Flower images.} We tried to keep the Flower images in colour, expecting colour to help recognise flowers. Surprisingly, validation accuracy decreased (from 83.5\% to 80.9\% for EfficientNet-B0), and even the accuracy on Flower did not improve. When we converted the Flower validation images to grayscale at test time, the colour model recognised only 26\% of them (vs.\ 90\% in colour), while the grayscale model was unaffected: the model learned the shortcut ``colour means Flower'' instead of what flowers look like. This shortcut would not hurt our test score, since Flower is also the only colour class in the test set, but it would fail on grayscale flowers. We learned that accuracy alone can hide such a shortcut.
